# Module 11: The Strategy Playbook

A field guide to the major ways people try to make money in stocks — what
each strategy believes, how it's executed, what the evidence says, and who
it actually suits. Read it to pick **one lane** (Module 7), not to run all of
them at once.

For each: **Thesis → Execution → Evidence → Fit.**

---

## 1. Passive indexing (the benchmark everything else must beat)

- **Thesis:** markets price stocks well enough that costs decide outcomes.
- **Execution:** total-market/S&P 500 funds, automatic buys, rebalance yearly.
- **Evidence:** overwhelming. ~90% of pros lose to it over 15 years.
- **Fit:** everyone, as the core. ~0 hours/week.

## 2. Value investing

- **Thesis:** markets overreact; statistically cheap stocks (low P/E, P/B,
  EV/EBIT) outperform over time as prices revert to value.
- **Execution:** screen for cheapness → eliminate value traps (dying
  businesses, debt bombs) via Module 4 analysis → diversify across 15–30
  names or use a value ETF → sell on re-rating.
- **Evidence:** strong over the last century (Graham → Buffett; Fama-French
  "value factor"), but with brutal decade-long droughts (2010–2020).
- **Fit:** patient, accounting-literate contrarians who can be "wrong"
  publicly for years.

## 3. Growth / quality-compounder investing

- **Thesis:** a few businesses compound earnings for decades (moats, high
  returns on capital); the market chronically *under*prices how long their
  growth lasts. "Our favorite holding period is forever."
- **Execution:** identify wide-moat, high-ROE, low-debt businesses (Module
  4's qualitative half) → pay a fair-not-crazy price → hold for years, add
  on selloffs → sell only when the moat cracks.
- **Evidence:** the "quality/profitability factor" is academically real;
  the risk is overpaying — great company ≠ great stock at any price
  (Cisco 2000 investors waited 20+ years).
- **Fit:** business-curious long-term holders. Few decisions, high patience.

## 4. Dividend / income investing

- **Thesis:** steady cash payouts enforce corporate discipline and provide
  returns you don't have to time.
- **Execution:** dividend-growth stocks or ETFs (SCHD, VIG) — favoring
  companies that *grow* payouts over decades ("Dividend Aristocrats") over
  the highest current yields (often traps: yield spikes because price
  crashed). Reinvest until you need income.
- **Evidence:** dividend *growers* have performed well; chasing raw yield
  hasn't. Total return (price + dividend) is what matters — a dividend is
  not extra money (Module 2).
- **Fit:** retirees and people who psychologically need cash flow to stay
  invested. Slightly tax-inefficient in taxable accounts.

## 5. Momentum / trend following

- **Thesis:** stocks that outperformed recently (3–12 months) keep doing so
  for a while — investor underreaction and herding create drift.
- **Execution (rules, not vibes):** rank universe by 6–12-month return →
  hold the top decile / or hold index only when above its 200-day MA →
  re-rank monthly → strict stops. ETFs exist (MTUM).
- **Evidence:** the strongest anomaly in academic finance ("premier
  anomaly" — Fama), documented across countries and centuries. The catch:
  high turnover (taxes!), and rare fast "momentum crashes" at bear-market
  turns (2009).
- **Fit:** systematic, unemotional rule-followers. Ruined by discretionary
  overrides.

## 6. Swing & day trading

Covered fully in Module 8. **Evidence:** ~97% of persistent day traders lose.
Treat as a skilled profession with a brutal failure rate, not a strategy.

## 7. GARP (growth at a reasonable price)

- **Thesis:** split the difference — growing companies at sane valuations.
- **Execution:** Peter Lynch's approach; the PEG ratio (Module 4) near/below
  1. Buy growth the market hasn't fully priced, often mid-caps you know
  from daily life — then verify with the numbers.
- **Evidence:** Lynch's Magellan fund did 29%/yr for 13 years; harder to
  systematize than pure value or momentum.
- **Fit:** stock-pickers who enjoy scuttlebutt research.

## 8. Sector rotation & macro

- **Thesis:** sectors lead in different cycle phases (early cycle: banks,
  small caps; late: energy, staples; recession: utilities, healthcare) —
  position for the phase.
- **Evidence:** the pattern is real in hindsight; *calling the phase in real
  time* is the hard part, and most tactical funds fail at it. This is where
  smart people most often outsmart themselves.
- **Fit:** honestly? Almost nobody as a core. Fine as a small tilt.

## 9. Event-driven (mergers, spinoffs, buybacks)

- **Thesis:** corporate events create forced/lazy sellers and mispricing.
  Spinoffs get dumped by index funds regardless of quality; merger targets
  trade below deal price (the spread = pay for deal-break risk).
- **Evidence:** spinoff outperformance is well documented (Greenblatt, *You
  Can Be a Stock Market Genius*); merger-arb is now dominated by pros.
- **Fit:** advanced hobbyists willing to read SEC filings for fun. Small
  part of a portfolio.

## 10. Copy/mirror strategies (superinvestors, insiders, Congress)

- **Thesis:** follow people with better information or skill.
- **Execution:** 13F filings reveal big funds' holdings quarterly (45-day
  lag); Form 4 shows corporate insider buys within 2 days (insider *buying*
  clusters are a genuinely studied bullish signal; selling means little);
  congressional trades — see **Module 12** for the full treatment.
- **Evidence:** mixed. Lags mean you buy after the move; you see positions,
  not reasoning, sizing context, or hedges; you won't get the sell signal
  in time. Insider-buy clusters have the best evidence of the three.
- **Fit:** as an *idea-generation* tool feeding your own Module 4 analysis —
  strong. As blind copying — weak.

## Comparison table

| Strategy | Effort | Turnover/taxes | Evidence | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Indexing | none | minimal | overwhelming | selling in panics |
| Value | high | low-med | strong, cyclical | value traps; giving up in droughts |
| Quality/growth | med | very low | good | overpaying; falling in love |
| Dividend growth | low-med | low | good | chasing yield |
| Momentum | med (systematic) | high | strongest anomaly | crashes; discretionary meddling |
| Day/swing trading | extreme | extreme | ~97% lose | all of Module 8 |
| GARP | high | low-med | good anecdotally | fake growth |
| Sector/macro | high | high | weak in real time | overconfidence |
| Event-driven | high | med | good in niches | complexity |
| Copy trading | low | med | mixed | stale info, no sell signal |

## How to choose

1. **Hours/week you'll honestly commit:** 0 → indexing. 1–3 → quality or
   dividend growth. 5–10 → value, GARP, momentum. 20+ → maybe trading.
2. **Temperament:** contrarian → value. Patient optimist → quality. Rule
   follower → momentum/systematic. Need income → dividends.
3. **Whatever you pick:** core/explore split (Module 3), written rules
   (Module 7), paper-trade the lane first, benchmark against VTI yearly.

The meta-lesson across every strategy that works: **the edge is less the
idea than the discipline to keep executing it through its inevitable bad
years.** Every strategy above has underperformed for 3–10 year stretches.
The people who got the published returns were the ones who didn't quit at
the bottom of the cycle.

## Checkpoint

- Which two strategies have the strongest academic evidence?
- Why does copying 13F filings underperform the funds being copied?
- Which lane fits your hours and temperament — and what's its historical
  worst stretch, so you know what you're signing up to sit through?

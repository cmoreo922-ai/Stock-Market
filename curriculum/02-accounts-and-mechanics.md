# Module 2: Accounts & Mechanics

## Brokerage accounts

You buy stocks through a **brokerage**. Major US brokers (Fidelity, Schwab,
Vanguard, and others) all offer $0 commissions on stock trades, no account
minimums, and fractional shares. Pick an established one; avoid apps that
gamify trading — confetti animations are not aligned with your interests.

### Account types (US)

| Type | Tax treatment | Best for |
|------|---------------|----------|
| **Taxable brokerage** | Pay taxes on dividends and realized gains | Flexibility; money you may need before retirement |
| **Traditional IRA / 401(k)** | Contributions may be pre-tax; taxed on withdrawal | Reducing taxes now |
| **Roth IRA / Roth 401(k)** | Contributions after-tax; growth and withdrawal tax-free | Most young investors — decades of tax-free compounding |

General priority order: capture any employer 401(k) match first (free money),
then Roth IRA, then more 401(k), then taxable.

## Order types

- **Market order** — "buy/sell now at the best available price." Executes
  immediately; the exact price isn't guaranteed. Fine for liquid large-cap
  stocks during market hours.
- **Limit order** — "buy at $X or lower / sell at $X or higher." Guarantees
  the price but not that it executes. Use for anything thinly traded, and any
  time in after-hours sessions.
- **Stop order (stop-loss)** — "if the price falls to $X, sell." Becomes a
  market order when triggered. Used to cap losses (see Module 6 for caveats).
- **Stop-limit order** — stop that becomes a limit order instead. Protects
  against selling into a crash gap, but might not execute at all.

Default habit worth building: **use limit orders.**

## Settlement, dividends, and splits

- Trades **settle** one business day after execution (T+1 in the US).
- Dividends: you must own the stock before the **ex-dividend date** to receive
  the payment. The stock price drops by roughly the dividend amount on that
  date — dividends are not free money.
- A **stock split** (e.g., 4-for-1) multiplies your shares and divides the
  price. Your position value doesn't change. Splits are cosmetic.

## Costs that actually matter

Commissions are gone, but costs remain:

1. **Expense ratios** on funds — an index fund charging 0.03%/yr vs. an active
   fund charging 1%/yr compounds to an enormous difference over decades.
2. **Bid-ask spreads** — pennies on large stocks, significant on small ones.
3. **Taxes** — see below.
4. **Your own overtrading** — the biggest cost for most people. Every trade is
   a chance to be wrong twice (selling the old thing, buying the new thing).

## Taxes (US basics)

- Sell at a profit in a taxable account → **capital gains tax**.
  - Held **≤ 1 year**: short-term gains, taxed as ordinary income (up to 37%).
  - Held **> 1 year**: long-term gains, taxed at 0%, 15%, or 20%.
  - This asymmetry alone is a strong argument for holding longer.
- **Qualified dividends** get long-term rates; ordinary dividends don't.
- Losses offset gains, plus up to $3,000/yr of ordinary income
  (**tax-loss harvesting**). Beware the **wash-sale rule**: repurchasing the
  same or a "substantially identical" security within 30 days disallows the
  loss deduction.
- None of this applies inside IRAs/401(k)s — no tax events until withdrawal
  (or never, for Roth).

## Margin and shorting: know what they are, then avoid them

- **Margin** = borrowing money from your broker to buy more stock. Amplifies
  gains *and* losses; a large enough drop triggers a **margin call**, forcing
  sales at the worst time. Losses can exceed your deposit.
- **Short selling** = borrowing shares to sell, hoping to buy back cheaper.
  Maximum gain 100%; maximum loss **unlimited**.
- **Options** deserve their own warning: most retail options buyers lose
  money, and complexity is where beginners get destroyed. Learn the basics
  eventually if curious; don't touch them for at least your first year.

Everything in this course can be done with a plain cash account.

# Module 10: Foreign & Global Markets

The US is roughly **60% of world stock-market value** — which means about 40%
of the world's investable businesses live elsewhere. This module covers what's
out there, how to buy it, and the risks that don't exist at home.

## The world's major markets

| Region | Key exchanges | Key indexes | Notes |
|--------|--------------|-------------|-------|
| **US** | NYSE, Nasdaq | S&P 500, Nasdaq, Russell 2000 | Deepest, most liquid, strongest shareholder protections |
| **UK** | London (LSE) | FTSE 100 | Global banks, energy, mining; historically high dividends |
| **Europe** | Euronext, Frankfurt (Deutsche Börse), SIX (Zurich) | STOXX 600, DAX 40, CAC 40 | Industrials, luxury (LVMH), pharma (Novartis, Roche), ASML |
| **Japan** | Tokyo (TSE) | Nikkei 225, TOPIX | World's #3 market; governance reforms since ~2023 revived returns |
| **China** | Shanghai, Shenzhen, Hong Kong (HKEX) | CSI 300, Hang Seng | Split between mainland A-shares (restricted) and HK/US listings |
| **India** | NSE, BSE | Nifty 50, Sensex | Fast-growing; direct access hard for foreigners — use funds |
| **Others** | Toronto (TSX), Sydney (ASX), Seoul (KRX), Taiwan (TWSE), São Paulo (B3) | — | Taiwan = TSMC; Korea = Samsung; Canada/Australia = resources & banks |

**Trading hours ripple around the globe**: Asia opens first, then Europe,
then the US. Overnight US futures react to what Asia/Europe did — this is why
stocks "gap" at the open (a headache for day traders, background noise for
investors).

## Developed vs. emerging markets

- **Developed** (Europe, Japan, Australia, Canada...): mature economies,
  strong rule of law. Benchmark: **MSCI EAFE**.
- **Emerging** (China, India, Brazil, Taiwan, Korea*, Mexico...): faster
  GDP growth, higher political/currency/governance risk. Benchmark:
  **MSCI Emerging Markets**. (*Korea/Taiwan are "emerging" by MSCI
  classification despite advanced economies — index politics.)
- Key lesson the last 30 years taught: **economic growth ≠ stock returns.**
  China grew GDP ~9%/yr for decades while its stock index went roughly
  nowhere. Returns come from earnings per share reaching *minority
  shareholders* — dilution, governance, and starting valuations matter more
  than GDP.

## How to actually buy foreign stocks (from the US)

1. **International ETFs — the right answer for almost everyone.**
   - VXUS / IXUS — total international (developed + emerging)
   - VEA / IEFA — developed only · VWO / IEMG — emerging only
   - Country funds exist (EWJ Japan, EWU UK, INDA India...) — concentrated
     bets, use sparingly.
   - Or one fund for everything including the US: VT (total world).
2. **ADRs (American Depositary Receipts)** — foreign shares wrapped to trade
   on US exchanges in dollars: Toyota (TM), ASML, Novo Nordisk (NVO), Alibaba
   (BABA). Convenient; watch for small custody fees and thinner liquidity in
   unsponsored ones.
3. **Direct foreign-exchange access** — some brokers (notably Interactive
   Brokers) let you trade Tokyo, London, etc. directly in local currency.
   For advanced users; adds currency conversion and foreign tax paperwork.

## Risks that don't exist at home

- **Currency risk** — a Japanese stock can rise 10% in yen while the yen
  falls 10% vs. the dollar: you made nothing. Currency swings often dominate
  short-term foreign returns. (Some funds "hedge" currency for a fee.)
- **Political / regulatory risk** — e.g., China's 2021 crackdown erased ~70%
  from its education sector overnight; Russia's market became untradeable to
  foreigners in 2022 — positions effectively went to zero regardless of the
  businesses.
- **Governance risk** — weaker disclosure and minority-shareholder
  protections in some markets; state-owned enterprises may serve the state
  first.
- **The VIE structure (China)** — most US-listed Chinese tech (Alibaba, etc.)
  is technically a contract-based shell, not direct ownership. It has held so
  far; know that it's what you own.
- **Withholding taxes** — many countries skim 10–30% of dividends. In US
  *taxable* accounts you can often reclaim via the foreign tax credit; in
  IRAs it's simply lost. (Mildly favors holding international funds in
  taxable accounts.)

## Why bother, then?

1. **The survivorship caveat**: US market history is the *best case*, not
   the average. Japan's Nikkei peaked in 1989 and took **34 years** to
   reclaim that high. A Japanese investor who "just bought the index" in
   1989 waited a working lifetime. Global diversification is insurance
   against your home market being the unlucky one.
2. **Valuation cycles**: US and international leadership alternates in long
   waves (international won 2002–2007; the US won 2010–2024). Buying only
   the recent winner is recency bias with extra steps.
3. **Exposure**: TSMC, ASML, Samsung, LVMH, Novo Nordisk, Toyota, Nestlé —
   world-dominant businesses that a US-only portfolio simply doesn't own.

Counterpoint honestly stated: S&P 500 companies already earn ~40% of revenue
abroad, and some great investors (Bogle, Buffett) considered US-only fine.
The mainstream recommendation is **20–40% of stocks in international funds**
— enough to matter, not enough to hurt if the US keeps winning.

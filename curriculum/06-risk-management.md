# Module 6: Risk Management

Making money in markets is less about brilliant picks than about **not getting
knocked out of the game**. Professionals obsess over risk first, returns
second. This module is the difference between investors who compound for
decades and traders who blow up in year two.

## The math of losses (memorize this)

Losses hurt more than gains help, asymmetrically:

| Loss | Gain needed to break even |
|------|---------------------------|
| −10% | +11% |
| −20% | +25% |
| −33% | +50% |
| −50% | +100% |
| −80% | +400% |

A 50% loss requires a *double* just to get back to even. This is why the first
rule of every great investor is some version of "don't lose big."

## Position sizing

**Never put so much into one idea that being wrong is catastrophic.**

- Common guideline for stock pickers: no single position over **5%** of your
  portfolio at cost (concentration through *growth* is a champagne problem).
- Traders often use the **1–2% risk rule**: risk no more than 1–2% of the
  account on any one trade. With a $10,000 account and a stop-loss 10% below
  entry, 1% risk means a $1,000 position ($10,000 × 1% ÷ 10%).
- Position size is your primary volume knob: high-conviction, well-understood,
  cheap → bigger (within limits); speculative → tiny or zero.

## Diversification

- Across **companies**: 20–30 stocks eliminates most single-company risk
  (index funds do this for you).
- Across **sectors**: ten tech stocks is one bet, not ten.
- Across **asset classes**: bonds and cash cushion stock crashes.
- Across **time**: dollar-cost averaging is diversification across entry
  points.

Concentration builds wealth for insiders who control the company;
diversification preserves wealth for everyone else.

## Stop-losses: uses and caveats

A stop-loss order automatically sells at a set price, capping a loss.

**For:** enforces discipline; caps single-trade damage; removes the "it'll
come back" trap.

**Against:** gaps can blow through stops (stock closes at $50, opens at $35 —
you sell at $35); ordinary volatility can stop you out of a good position
right before it recovers; long-term investors in index funds generally
shouldn't use them at all — for them, drops are buying opportunities.

Sensible use: stops for shorter-term *trades* (placed below a logical support
level, sized so the loss is 1–2% of the account); written re-evaluation
triggers rather than automatic stops for long-term *investments*.

## Psychology: the actual boss battle

Nearly every catastrophic outcome is behavioral, not analytical.

- **Loss aversion** → holding losers ("it's not a loss until I sell") and
  selling winners too early. The market doesn't know your entry price.
- **FOMO** → buying what already skyrocketed, near tops, because everyone's
  talking about it. If your barber is recommending it, the easy money is gone.
- **Overconfidence** → after a few wins (especially in a bull market where
  everything rises), mistaking luck for skill and sizing up right before the
  lesson arrives.
- **Confirmation bias** → reading only bullish takes on stocks you own.
  Actively seek the bear case for every position.
- **Revenge trading** → trying to win back a loss immediately with a bigger
  bet. This is how accounts die.

**Defenses:** written rules made in calm moments (Module 7), position-size
limits, a trade journal, and automation (scheduled index buys can't panic).

## Portfolio-level protection

1. **Emergency fund first** — 3–6 months of expenses in cash, always. Forced
   selling during a crash is how paper losses become real ones.
2. **No leverage** — margin converts temporary declines into permanent losses.
3. **Age/timeline-appropriate allocation** — money needed within 5 years
   doesn't belong in stocks. Period.
4. **Rebalance** on a schedule (e.g., yearly): trim what grew, add to what
   shrank. It mechanically sells high and buys low.

## The risk checklist before any trade

- What % of my portfolio is this position?
- What exactly makes me exit — at what price or on what news, and what's the
  maximum I'd lose there?
- Is that maximum loss ≤ 1–2% of my account (trades) or an amount I can
  genuinely shrug off (investments)?
- Am I acting on a written plan, or on an emotion I picked up today?

If any answer is fuzzy, the position is too big or the plan isn't ready.

## Checkpoint

- Why does a 50% loss need a 100% gain to recover?
- Compute: $25,000 account, 1% risk rule, stop 8% below entry — what's the
  max position size? (Answer: $3,125.)
- Which behavioral bias do you think *you* are most vulnerable to, and what
  rule will you write to counter it?

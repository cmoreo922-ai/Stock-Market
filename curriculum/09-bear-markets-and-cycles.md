# Module 9: Bear Markets, Crashes & Market Cycles

Bear markets are where long-term wealth is actually determined — not because
of what the market does, but because of what *you* do. Everyone is a genius
in a bull market; the compounding you keep is decided in the bad years.

## Vocabulary of a downturn

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Pullback** | −5% to −10% from a high. Happens most years, several times. |
| **Correction** | −10% to −20%. Happens roughly every 1–2 years. |
| **Bear market** | −20% or worse. Happens roughly every 5–7 years on average. |
| **Crash** | A very fast decline (days/weeks), e.g., 1987, March 2020. |
| **Recession** | Economic contraction (shrinking GDP, rising unemployment). Often overlaps bear markets, but not always. |
| **Capitulation** | The panic-selling climax where "everyone gives up" — often near bottoms. |
| **Dead-cat bounce** | A sharp temporary rally inside an ongoing downtrend. |

## The historical record (S&P 500)

| Bear market | Peak-to-trough | Duration | Time to recover |
|-------------|---------------|----------|-----------------|
| 1973–74 (oil shock, inflation) | −48% | 21 mo | ~6 yrs nominal |
| 1987 (Black Monday crash) | −34% | 3 mo | ~2 yrs |
| 2000–02 (dot-com bust) | −49% | 31 mo | ~7 yrs |
| 2007–09 (financial crisis) | −57% | 17 mo | ~5.5 yrs |
| 2020 (COVID) | −34% | 1 mo | 5 mo |
| 2022 (inflation/rates) | −25% | 9 mo | ~2 yrs |

Patterns worth internalizing:

- Declines of 30–50% are **normal features** of equity investing, not
  anomalies. You will live through several more.
- Every single one recovered to new highs. (US history; see Module 10 for
  the honest caveat that not every *country's* market has been so kind.)
- The best single days cluster **inside** bear markets. Missing just the 10
  best days over 30 years roughly halves your total return — and they tend
  to land within days of the worst ones. This is why "sell now, get back in
  when it's calmer" reliably fails: calm returns *after* the rebound.
- Markets bottom **before** the economy improves — typically 4–6 months
  before recession-end. Waiting for good news means buying much higher.

## The anatomy of a cycle

Classic sequence (stylized, not a timing tool):

1. **Early bull:** pessimism everywhere, valuations cheap, nobody believes
   the rally. Best returns happen here.
2. **Mid bull:** economy visibly improving, earnings growing, steady gains.
3. **Late bull / euphoria:** IPO manias, "this time is different," your
   most risk-averse friends start buying speculative assets, leverage grows.
4. **The top:** rarely announced by anything. Usually only visible in
   hindsight.
5. **Bear:** denial → fear → capitulation. Valuations overshoot downward.
6. Repeat.

**Interest rates** drive much of this: when central banks (the Fed) raise
rates, borrowing costs rise, future profits are worth less today, and stocks
— especially expensive growth stocks — get repriced downward (2022 was a
textbook case). Rate *cuts* generally support stocks, unless they're cuts
into a recession.

**Yield-curve inversions** (short rates above long rates) have preceded most
US recessions, with long and variable lags — famous as a signal, useless as
a timing device.

## What to actually do in a bear market

For the index core (Modules 3, 7):

1. **Nothing different.** Continue automatic buys. Every purchase during a
   bear market is buying the same businesses at a discount.
2. **Rebalance** — mechanically moves money from bonds/cash into cheap
   stocks.
3. **Tax-loss harvest** in taxable accounts — sell losers, immediately buy
   a *similar but not identical* fund (avoid wash sales), bank the deduction,
   stay invested.
4. **Do not check the account daily.** Volatility is emotional radiation;
   limit exposure.

For stock pickers:

- Bear markets are when wonderful businesses go on sale. Work your Module 4
  checklist with extra weight on **balance-sheet survival** — leverage kills
  in downturns.
- Avoid catching falling knives on broken businesses; "down 80%" often
  precedes "down 95%." Cheapness is measured against fundamentals, not
  against the old price.

For traders:

- Bear-market rallies are violent in both directions; position sizes should
  shrink, not grow. Many pros simply trade less or sit out.
- Shorting bear markets sounds obvious and is brutally hard in practice —
  the sharpest upward days of all occur inside downtrends.

## Hedging tools (know they exist; use sparingly)

- **Cash/bonds allocation** — the honest hedge. Boring, reliable.
- **Put options** — insurance that usually expires worthless; a permanent
  drag if bought habitually.
- **Inverse ETFs (SH, etc.)** — for day/short-term use only; daily rebalancing
  makes them decay badly when held long.
- **VIX products** — the "fear index" itself isn't investable without severe
  decay. Leave these to professionals.

For almost everyone, the right hedge is an allocation you can hold through a
−50% and an emergency fund so you're never a forced seller.

## Write your bear-market plan now

In your IPS (Module 7), in calm conditions, commit to answers for:

- At −20%, I will: ______
- At −35%, I will: ______
- At −50%, I will: ______
- I will know my thesis is wrong (vs. just early) if: ______

The historical answer that made money in every US bear market to date:
*keep buying on schedule, rebalance, harvest losses, and wait.*

# Module 1: Foundations

## What a stock actually is

A share of stock is partial ownership of a real business. If a company has
1 million shares and you own 100, you own 0.01% of that company — a claim on
0.01% of its future profits and assets.

This is the single most important idea in investing: **you are buying a piece
of a business, not a lottery ticket or a blinking number.** Everything else in
this course builds on it.

## How you make money with stocks

There are exactly two ways:

1. **Price appreciation** — you sell shares for more than you paid.
2. **Dividends** — the company pays out part of its profits to shareholders,
   usually quarterly.

Over long periods, stock prices tend to follow business fundamentals: a company
that grows its earnings tends to see its stock rise. Over short periods (days,
weeks, even a year or two), prices move on news, emotion, and supply/demand —
which is why short-term trading is much harder than it looks.

## How the market works

- **Exchanges** (NYSE, Nasdaq) are marketplaces matching buyers and sellers.
- A stock's **price** is simply the last price at which a buyer and seller
  agreed to trade. There is no "official" value — just an ongoing auction.
- The **bid** is the highest price anyone will currently pay; the **ask** is
  the lowest price anyone will currently sell for. The gap is the **spread**.
- Regular US market hours are 9:30 AM – 4:00 PM Eastern, Monday–Friday.

## Market indexes

An **index** measures the market's overall level:

- **S&P 500** — the 500 largest US companies. The default benchmark. When
  people say "the market," they usually mean this.
- **Dow Jones Industrial Average** — 30 large companies. Old and famous, but
  a worse gauge than the S&P.
- **Nasdaq Composite** — heavy on technology companies.

Historically the S&P 500 has returned roughly **10% per year on average**
before inflation (about 7% after) — but with huge variation: individual years
have ranged from about −37% to +38%. Averages hide the ride.

## Compounding: the reason to start early

Returns earn returns. $10,000 at 7% per year:

| Years | Value |
|-------|-------|
| 10 | ~$19,700 |
| 20 | ~$38,700 |
| 30 | ~$76,100 |
| 40 | ~$149,700 |

The last decade adds more than the first three combined. Time in the market is
the most powerful variable you control — more than picking the right stocks.

## The uncomfortable truths (read these twice)

1. **Most professionals lose to the index.** Over 15-year periods, roughly
   90% of actively managed US stock funds have underperformed the S&P 500.
2. **Most day traders lose money.** Studies across multiple countries
   consistently find the large majority of frequent traders lose, after costs.
3. **Nobody reliably predicts short-term prices.** Not analysts, not
   newsletters, not people on social media — and especially not people
   selling you a course on how to do it.
4. **If someone guarantees returns, it's a scam.** Full stop.

None of this means you can't make money in stocks — it means the reliable way
looks boring (Module 3), and beating the market is a serious skill that takes
years to develop, if it develops at all.

- What are the two ways a stock makes you money?
- What does the S&P 500 measure, and what has it returned historically?
- Why does starting 10 years earlier matter so much?
- What fraction of professional fund managers beat the index long-term?

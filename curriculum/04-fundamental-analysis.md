# Module 4: Fundamental Analysis

Fundamental analysis means valuing a stock by studying the underlying
business: what it earns, what it owns, what it owes, and where it's heading.
The premise: price and value can diverge in the short run, but converge over
time — so buying good businesses below their value should pay off.

## The three financial statements

Every public company files these quarterly (10-Q) and annually (10-K), free at
sec.gov or the company's investor-relations page.

### 1. Income statement — "How much did it earn?"

Top to bottom:

- **Revenue** (sales) — money in from customers.
- **Gross profit** — revenue minus the direct cost of goods sold.
- **Operating income** — after salaries, R&D, marketing, etc.
- **Net income** — the bottom line, after interest and taxes.
- **EPS (earnings per share)** — net income ÷ shares outstanding.

What to look for: is revenue *growing*? Are margins (profit ÷ revenue) stable
or expanding? A company growing revenue 15%/yr with expanding margins is a
very different animal from one shrinking 5%/yr.

### 2. Balance sheet — "What does it own and owe?"

- **Assets** (cash, inventory, property) = **Liabilities** (debt, payables)
  + **Shareholders' equity**.
- Key check: **debt vs. cash**. A company with $50B debt and $2B in annual
  profit is fragile; one with net cash is resilient.

### 3. Cash flow statement — "Where did the cash actually go?"

Earnings can be massaged with accounting; cash is harder to fake.

- **Operating cash flow** — cash generated by the actual business.
- **Free cash flow (FCF)** = operating cash flow − capital expenditures.
  The cash truly available to shareholders. Many professionals value
  companies primarily on FCF.

Red flag: net income rising while operating cash flow falls.

## The key ratios

| Ratio | Formula | What it tells you |
|-------|---------|-------------------|
| **P/E** | Price ÷ EPS | How many years of current earnings you're paying for. S&P 500 historical average ≈ 15–20. |
| **Forward P/E** | Price ÷ next-year expected EPS | Same, using analyst estimates (which are often wrong). |
| **PEG** | P/E ÷ earnings growth rate | Adjusts P/E for growth; ~1 is classically "fair." |
| **P/S** | Market cap ÷ revenue | Useful for unprofitable growth companies. |
| **P/B** | Price ÷ book value | Useful for banks and asset-heavy firms. |
| **ROE** | Net income ÷ equity | How efficiently the company turns capital into profit. >15% sustained is strong. |
| **Debt/Equity** | Total debt ÷ equity | Leverage. Compare within an industry. |
| **Dividend yield** | Annual dividend ÷ price | Cash return. Suspiciously high yield often signals danger. |

**Rules for using ratios:**

1. Compare within the same industry — a 30 P/E is normal for software and
   alarming for a utility.
2. Compare against the company's own history.
3. A low P/E is not automatically cheap. Dying businesses have low P/Es
   (the **value trap**). A high P/E is not automatically expensive if growth
   is real and durable.

## Qualitative factors (the part ratios can't capture)

- **Moat** — a durable competitive advantage: network effects, switching
  costs, brand, scale, patents. Ask: *what stops competitors from taking
  this company's profits?* If the answer is "nothing," high margins won't last.
- **Management** — do they own stock? Is their guidance historically honest?
  Do they buy back shares cheaply or empire-build with overpriced acquisitions?
- **Industry trend** — a great company in a shrinking industry is swimming
  upstream.

## A simple analysis checklist

For any stock you're considering, answer in writing:

1. What does this company sell, and to whom? (If you can't explain it in two
   sentences, stop here.)
2. Is revenue growing? Are margins stable or improving?
3. Does it generate positive, growing free cash flow?
4. Could it survive a bad recession? (Check debt vs. cash.)
5. What is its moat, specifically?
6. Is the valuation reasonable vs. peers and its own history?
7. **Why is the market wrong about this stock?** — You're buying because you
   think the price is too low. Someone informed is selling to you. What do
   you see that they don't? If you have no answer, you have no edge — buy the
   index instead.

## Checkpoint

- Which statement shows free cash flow, and why do professionals trust cash
  over earnings?
- Why can a low P/E stock be a bad deal?
- What is a moat? Name a real company you believe has one and say why.

# Module 3: Index Investing First

This is the shortest module and the one most likely to actually make you money.

## The core argument

- The stock market as a whole has grown ~10%/yr on average for a century.
- Roughly 90% of professional fund managers fail to beat it over 15 years —
  and you can't identify the winning 10% in advance.
- Therefore: **owning the whole market at near-zero cost is the highest
  expected-value strategy available to a normal person.**

An **index fund** does exactly that. One purchase of an S&P 500 or total-market
fund makes you a part-owner of hundreds of businesses at once, with expense
ratios as low as 0.02–0.05% per year.

Examples of the category (not recommendations): VOO / SPY / IVV track the
S&P 500; VTI tracks the total US market; VT tracks the whole world. Any major
broker's equivalent is fine — what matters is **broad diversification and a
rock-bottom expense ratio**.

## ETFs vs. mutual funds

Both can track the same index. **ETFs** trade like stocks during market hours
and are slightly more tax-efficient in taxable accounts. **Index mutual funds**
trade once daily at closing value and make automatic investing easy. Either is
fine; the expense ratio matters more than the wrapper.

## Dollar-cost averaging (DCA)

Invest a fixed amount on a fixed schedule — say, $500 on the 1st of every
month — regardless of what the market is doing.

Why it works for humans:

- Removes the temptation to time the market (which fails empirically).
- You automatically buy more shares when prices are low, fewer when high.
- It turns investing into a habit instead of a series of stressful decisions.

Mathematically, investing a lump sum immediately beats DCA about two-thirds of
the time (markets rise more often than fall) — but DCA beats *not investing
because you're waiting for a dip*, which is what most people actually do.

## The behavior gap

Index funds only work if you **don't sell during crashes**. The market has
dropped 30–50% several times (2000–02, 2008–09, 2020, 2022) and recovered to
new highs every time. The investors who got hurt were overwhelmingly the ones
who sold near the bottom.

Fund investors as a group earn 1–2%/yr *less* than the very funds they own,
purely from buying high and selling low. Closing this "behavior gap" is worth
more than any stock-picking skill.

Rules that protect you from yourself:

1. Only invest money you won't need for 5+ years.
2. Keep an emergency fund (3–6 months of expenses) in cash first.
3. Write down, today, what you'll do when the market drops 30%. (Correct
   answer: nothing, or buy more.)

## A complete starter portfolio

This one-line portfolio outperforms most professionals over time:

> **Every month, buy a total-market or S&P 500 index fund. Never sell until
> you need the money. That's it.**

Add international exposure (e.g., 20–40% in a total international fund) and,
as you approach the time you'll need the money, bonds. Complexity beyond that
mostly adds cost, not return.

## "But this is boring — I want to pick stocks"

Fine — that's what the rest of this course is for. But do it in this structure:

- **Core (90%):** index funds, automated monthly.
- **Explore (≤10%):** individual stock picks, using Modules 4–7.

The core guarantees you compound with the market for decades. The explore
sliver lets you learn stock-picking with stakes small enough that mistakes are
tuition, not catastrophe. If your picks beat the index for several years
running, you've earned the right to raise the sliver.

## Checkpoint

- Why does a 0.03% expense ratio beat a 1% one so badly over 30 years?
  (Run the numbers: 7% vs. 6% growth on $10k for 30 years.)
- What is the behavior gap, and what causes it?
- What is the core/explore split, and what problem does it solve?
